Cells Explorer

Every living thing is made of cells. Tap any part of the animal cell or the plant cell to see what it does, then find out how cells build tissues, organs and whole organisms. Ages 11-14.

Specialised cells

Your body has about 200 different types of cell. Most of them are specialised: each has a shape and features that help it to do one job. These features are called adaptations.

Red blood cell

Carries oxygen
  • No nucleus, so there is more room for haemoglobin, the red substance that carries oxygen.
  • Its dimpled disc shape gives it a large surface area for taking in oxygen.

Nerve cell

Carries electrical signals
  • Very long, so it can carry signals from one part of the body to another.
  • Branched ends connect it to many other nerve cells.

Sperm cell

Carries genetic material to the egg cell
  • A tail for swimming.
  • Lots of mitochondria to release the energy it needs to swim.

Ciliated cell

Keeps the airways clean
  • Tiny hairs called cilia on its top surface.
  • The cilia wave to sweep mucus, dust and microbes away from the lungs.

Root hair cell

Takes in water and minerals from the soil
  • A long, thin hair gives it a large surface area.
  • No chloroplasts, because there is no light underground.

Palisade cell

Makes food by photosynthesis
  • Packed with chloroplasts.
  • Found near the top of a leaf, where there is the most light.

Three extra parts

Make food by photosynthesis

Why the difference?

Plants make their own food
Food
A plant cannot move to find food, so it makes it in its chloroplasts
Support
A plant has no skeleton, so cell walls and full vacuoles hold it up

Looking at cells: the microscope

Cells are too small to see with your eyes alone. A light microscope uses two lenses to make them look bigger. Choose an objective lens to see what happens to these onion cells.

Living things made of one cell

You are multicellular, which means you are made of many cells. Some living things are unicellular: the whole organism is a single cell that does every job itself.

Getting in and out: diffusion

Substances move into and out of a cell through the cell membrane by diffusion. The particles spread out from where there are a lot of them (a high concentration) to where there are fewer (a low concentration).

Into the cell

Oxygen and glucose
Why
The mitochondria need them for respiration

Out of the cell

Carbon dioxide
Why
It is a waste product of respiration

Robert Hooke was the first person to describe cells. In 1665 he looked at a thin slice of cork through a microscope and saw rows of tiny boxes. He called them cells.
